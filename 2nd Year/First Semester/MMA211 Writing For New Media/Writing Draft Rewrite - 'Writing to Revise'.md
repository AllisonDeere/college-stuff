Writing can be a long and painful endeavor, when it is not done as a structured and logical process. The first step of writing anything, before grammar and revisions, is to *Freewrite*. Essentially, it's just dumping all of the ideas and thoughts a writer has in their head without any structure nor necessarily any rules. There are no wrong thoughts in freewriting, as it is the step a writer takes to focus getting anything, no matter how silly the idea may be, on a page to be revised and improved on later. Compartmentalization is the key here; keeping each process, from the birth of the idea to the later improved version of the page is extremely beneficial. This allows you, as the writer, to focus on simply letting your thoughts run wild first, and making those thoughts make sense later.

Of course, most ideas don't just come out of thin air. Our brains can only hold so much information, and many times that information is lost in a sea of noise. Many great authors; Hemingway, Lovecraft, and McCarthy; were all influenced and drew inspiration from their predecessors. Even in a non-fictional setting, such as this very blog, there are great benefits from consuming media or other articles to get a taste of a fresh idea. In the process of freewriting, emptying your own mental library can be detrimental rather than helpful; fresh ideas are scarce and therefore finding inspiration for one is both easier and exposes you to new ideas worth taking note of.

Motivation is one of the many reasons for doing this whole process. For most people, motivation rarely comes at a single "Aha!" moment. Most often, it comes from action; we must keep moving, even when we are only starting. It is better to have an unreadable draft that you are able to edit and improve on later, than a perfect idea that has not come into fruition.  

For a thought experiment, let's imagine a new, unfurnished home that you just bought. The house is desolate and empty, but you have been given an unlimited amount of furniture and appliances. The smart way to arrange your house to look appealing to your taste would be to dump a truck-load of furniture in every room, and only then should you arrange and re-arrange the furniture how you see fit. This scenario can be applied to writing, too. Freewriting was the act of taking our ideas without rhythm, grammar, structure, or even illogical connections and factual biases, and dumping all of that in a page. Now that everything is in it's place, we can start to re-arrange our ideas how we see fit.

Before we proceed to revision, however, we must take a break. Whether it be a short rest or multiple days, even a small period away from our draft is beneficial to gain an objective and fresh perspective. Sleeping, relaxing, or simply doing something else allows the brain to see your work better once you return to your text. In a way, it's like seeing another person's work-- only that that person is your past self.

The next step of the process is revision. Revision or 'Editing' is the act of trimming, rearranging, or even completely removing thoughts and sentences from your work. Let's go back to our thought experiment: You're finished with moving the sofa to the living room, but now you realize what was meant to be the kitchen from your initial idea would look so much better as a bedroom. So, you move the fridge, counters, and oven to the new room that would ultimately turn to be a kitchen. Revision is exactly this. If in Freewriting we were simply solving the problem of *starting*, revising is solving the problem of *logic* and *communication*. 

The final part of editing is called *proofreading*. You've moved all the furniture to where it belongs, so now it's time to review and check if the spots that you've placed your furniture in look appealing. Same with our text: grammar and spelling first, then structure and form last. Many of our grammatical errors occur *during* editing, as while we rearrange or delete sections, we may have a recurring "the" or miss words completely. The best way to proofread is to read out loud, as verbally speaking your text will lead you to notice the *hiccups* and errors in your writing faster.  

Essentially, writing isn't about producing a perfect piece of work on the first attempt, but to allow those ideas to flow, develop, and improve through a structured process. Freewriting gives those ideas a place to first exist, revision allows them to be given clarity and purpose, and finally, proofreading ensures that your text has been communicated effectively in the final version of your work. Like furnishing an empty house, good writing is built upon experimentation, re-arrangement, refinement, and compartmentalization. By following through and separating each and every stage of the process, writing becomes less intimidating and more manageable, by turning scatterbrained thoughts into something worth reading.
